On October 30, 2024, exactly six days before Americans cast ballots in the presidential election, the Supreme Court issued an unsigned order with no written explanation. By a 6-3 party-line vote, the conservative majority reinstated Virginia's eleventh-hour voter purge program, overruling both a federal district court and a unanimous panel of the Fourth U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals.

The ruling allowed the state to strip roughly 1,600 people from the voter rolls on the eve of the election. But the most alarming aspect of the Court's intervention wasn't merely the timing or the outcome. It was that Congress had explicitly passed a federal statute thirty-one years earlier specifically forbidding states from doing what Virginia did — and six Supreme Court justices erased that protection in two sentences without bothering to explain why.

<!--more-->

## The 90-Day "Quiet Period" Congress Put in Law

In 1993, Congress passed the National Voter Registration Act (NVRA). The statute is best known for the "Motor Voter" provision requiring state DMVs to offer voter registration opportunities, but lawmakers understood that expanding access meant little if states could arbitrarily strip voters from the rolls right before an election.

To prevent that exact scenario, Congress wrote Section 8(c)(2) of the NVRA into federal law:

> "A State shall complete, not later than 90 days prior to the date of a primary or general election for Federal office, any program the purpose of which is to systematically remove the names of ineligible voters from the official lists of eligible voters."

The legislative rationale was straightforward and sensible: automated, mass-matching government programs are notoriously error-prone. If an election official conducts a systematic purge in March, an eligible voter who is erroneously flagged has months to receive notice, provide documentation, or correct bureaucratic errors. But if a state conducts mass removals in October, voters only find out they have been disenfranchised when poll workers turn them away on Election Day.

Congress did not create an exception for purges aimed at noncitizens. It established a hard, bright-line 90-day "quiet period" for *all* systematic list maintenance. States can still remove individual voters based on individualized evidence — if someone dies, moves out of state, or directly asks to be removed — but sweeping, algorithmically driven removals must stop 90 days before the vote.

On August 7, 2024 — literally exactly 90 days before the November 5 presidential election — Virginia Governor Glenn Youngkin issued Executive Order 35. The order created a daily automated process matching Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) records against voter registration rolls to identify and cancel the registrations of suspected noncitizens.

## "I'm Not Dealing With Beliefs. I'm Dealing With Evidence."

When civil rights groups and the U.S. Department of Justice sued to halt the program, the state defended itself by claiming it was merely removing noncitizens who were never legally eligible to vote in the first place.

The trouble with that defense was the evidence.

State DMV records are static snapshots in time, not live citizenship tracking databases. If an immigrant obtains a Virginia driver's license while on a student visa or green card, DMV databases record that person as a noncitizen. When that person becomes a naturalized U.S. citizen three or five years later, they are constitutionally entitled to register and vote — yet the state's automated system looked backward at outdated DMV paperwork and flagged them for removal. Native-born citizens who accidentally marked a poorly designed checkbox on a bureaucratic renewal form years earlier were swept into the same automated dragnet.

The case landed before U.S. District Judge Patricia Tolliver Giles in the Eastern District of Virginia. Over days of detailed hearings, Judge Giles pressed the state to provide proof that the 1,600 individuals it removed were actually noncitizens. Virginia's lawyers admitted they had not verified the current citizenship status of the purged individuals, relying instead on automated database matches and bureaucratic silence when warning letters were mailed.

When state attorneys repeatedly asserted their belief that the purged voters were noncitizens, Judge Giles stopped them cold:

> *"I'm not dealing with beliefs. I'm dealing with evidence."*

The evidence showed that legitimate, naturalized, and native-born American citizens had their voting rights cancelled under the program. On October 25, 2024, Judge Giles issued a preliminary injunction ordering Virginia to immediately restore the roughly 1,600 voters to the rolls and mail corrective notices confirming their right to vote. Two days later, a three-judge panel of the Fourth Circuit unanimously affirmed her ruling.

Both lower courts applied the law as written: Virginia had instituted an automated, systematic removal program during the statutory 90-day quiet period, in direct violation of the NVRA.

## The One-Way Ratchet of the Purcell Principle

Then Virginia filed an emergency application to the Supreme Court — and the conservative majority deployed its favorite emergency weapon.

For nearly two decades, the Supreme Court has governed last-minute election litigation under the *Purcell* principle, derived from the 2006 case *Purcell v. Gonzalez*. The ostensible doctrine is that federal courts should not alter election rules in the final weeks before voting begins because late judicial changes risk causing "voter confusion" and undermining confidence in the administration of the election.

Conservative justices have repeatedly invoked *Purcell* to strike down lower-court orders that expanded ballot drop boxes, extended mail-in deadlines during natural disasters, or blocked restrictive voting laws. Whenever voting rights advocates asked federal courts to protect access close to an election, the majority's response was predictable: *It's too close to the election to change the rules.*

Yet look at what happened in [*Beals v. Virginia Coalition for Immigrant Rights*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/24pdf/24a407_k536.pdf):

1. **The state** altered the rules inside the 90-day window by introducing a new daily automated purge system.
2. **The district court** did not invent a new election procedure; it simply enforced a 31-year-old federal statute designed specifically to maintain the status quo during the final 90 days.
3. **The Supreme Court** stepped in *six days before the election* to overturn the lower courts, halt the restoration of eligible citizens, and permit the state's last-minute purge to stand.

In the majority's hands, the *Purcell* principle was turned completely on its head. When a lower court protects voters under federal law, the Supreme Court cries "last-minute disruption." But when a state executes a last-minute voter purge in direct violation of federal statute, the Court uses its emergency docket to protect the purge. 

The doctrine has become a one-way ratchet: it bars courts from stopping voter suppression, but never stops states from executing it.

## The Darkness of the Shadow Docket

The Supreme Court did not bother to reconcile its decision with the text of the National Voter Registration Act. It did not explain how Section 8(c)(2)'s explicit 90-day quiet period could be set aside. It did not address Judge Giles's factual findings that eligible American citizens were stripped of their ballot.

It simply issued an order: stay granted. Six justices voted to greenlight the purge; Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Elena Kagan, and Ketanji Brown Jackson noted their dissent.

This is the emergency "shadow docket" at its most destructive — [the exact tool Justices Kagan and Barrett admitted under oath lacks coherent standards](/posts/kagan-barrett-shadow-docket-testimony/). Major questions of federal statutory law and constitutional voting rights are decided without full briefing, without oral argument, and without an opinion explaining the majority's legal theory.

When an appellate court reverses a lower court with no written rationale, it isn't practicing jurisprudence. It is exercising raw administrative fiat. It tells the public and lower-court judges that the written law passed by Congress is secondary to the political preferences of five or six individuals wearing black robes.

## Why This Proves the Case for Structural Reform

The Virginia voter purge order wasn't an isolated procedural quirk. It fits an escalating pattern where the Supreme Court's majority uses its emergency docket to bypass standard legal scrutiny, tilt electoral rules, and insulate state-level voting restrictions from accountability.

This is why ethics reforms and financial disclosure rules, while necessary, will never fix the Supreme Court's underlying crisis. A justice who discloses their private jet flights will still vote to gut the National Voter Registration Act on the shadow docket six days before an election. An advisory ethics code will not stop a 6-3 majority from converting the *Purcell* principle into a partisan shield.

As long as an unaccountable supermajority can reshape the electorate in the dark with unreasoned emergency orders, the fundamental right to vote remains at the mercy of judicial whim. Protecting American democracy requires [systemic, structural reform](/posts/comprehensive-reform-needed/) — enforceable term limits, emergency docket transparency mandates, and jurisdiction stripping that removes the Court's ability to nullify voting rights protections passed by Congress.

Until those reforms are enacted, the law of elections isn't what Congress writes in statute. It's whatever six justices decide to let stand in the middle of the night.
