On the morning of September 30, 2026, a federal appeals court did something courts are supposed to do when a person is hours from being put to death: it paused to think.

By evening, the Supreme Court had told the appeals court to stop thinking. Its [order in *Nelsen v. Pike*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/26A428) (No. 26A428) contained no reasoning. Tennessee then tried to execute Christa Pike by lethal injection, and the attempt failed. Pike, 50, survived.

The sequence is a case study in what is wrong with how the Supreme Court handles life-and-death questions on its emergency docket.

<!--more-->

## A Short Stay, Immediately Erased

Pike was convicted of killing Colleen Slemmer, a 19-year-old classmate at a Knoxville Job Corps center, in 1995. Pike was 18 at the time. She does not deny the killing. Her lawyers argue that she had untreated mental illness and a childhood marked by sexual abuse that began in toddlerhood, with rapes at ages 11 and 17, and that she was later diagnosed with bipolar disorder and PTSD ([WPLN](https://wpln.org/post/tennessee-set-to-carry-out-rare-execution-of-a-woman/)).

Governor Bill Lee denied clemency on Monday, September 28. The Supreme Court rejected Pike's application to halt the execution the next day ([WPLN](https://wpln.org/post/tennessee-set-to-carry-out-rare-execution-of-a-woman/)).

On Wednesday morning, a divided panel of the Sixth Circuit, by a 2-1 vote, entered a short stay, "until further order of this court," in case No. 26-5864 ([Law Dork](https://www.lawdork.com/p/the-big-problems-with-wednesdays)). Its stated purpose was to "adequately address and resolve" the issues Pike raised, including whether her abuse history had been fully weighed at sentencing ([WPLN](https://wpln.org/post/tennessee-set-to-carry-out-rare-execution-of-a-woman/)). A dissenting judge called it "another last-ditch meritless effort to delay a lawful execution" ([MS NOW](https://www.ms.now/deadline-white-house/deadline-legal-blog/christa-pike-execution-supreme-court-ptsd-tennessee)).

Tennessee appealed immediately, calling the stay an unjust reward for Pike's "gamesmanship" ([MS NOW](https://www.ms.now/deadline-white-house/deadline-legal-blog/christa-pike-execution-supreme-court-ptsd-tennessee)). The Supreme Court [granted the application and vacated the stay](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/26A428). The majority gave no explanation. The three Democratic appointees dissented.

## "Stunningly, the Court Acquiesces"

Justice Sotomayor wrote the [dissent](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/26A428), joined by Justices Kagan and Jackson. "I see no reason to short-circuit the ordinary process of appellate review," she wrote, adding that the Court "chooses to overlook that principle precisely when it matters most." She invoked the Court's own maxim that it is "a court of review, not of first view." She also wrote that the order "clears the way for the State to execute Pike before her still-pending legal challenges" can be considered ([Law Dork](https://www.lawdork.com/p/the-big-problems-with-wednesdays)).

That maxim matters. The Supreme Court routinely tells litigants it will not decide questions the lower courts have not yet fully addressed. Here, a lower court was in the middle of addressing one, and the Court cut it off, in a capital case, with an unsigned order that said nothing.

Whether Pike's claim would have succeeded is not the point. Pike's claim, backed by clinical evidence from her lawyers, was that her PTSD "would be profoundly triggered before and during the execution process" ([MS NOW](https://www.ms.now/deadline-white-house/deadline-legal-blog/christa-pike-execution-supreme-court-ptsd-tennessee)). That is a novel Eighth Amendment question. Novel questions are exactly what appellate courts exist to consider. The Sixth Circuit asked for a short pause. It was not given one.

## The Execution That Did Not Work

What followed is hard to read.

According to witnesses quoted by [WPLN](https://wpln.org/post/tennessee-set-to-carry-out-rare-execution-of-a-woman/), Tennessee officials appeared to administer two doses of pentobarbital. At 7:46 p.m., the curtain to the media room was closed. It was raised about three minutes later, and Pike could be heard snoring. The curtain closed again at 8:06 p.m. Witnesses could not see the procedure, but they heard groans, moans and snoring for more than 40 minutes before the microphone was cut off and they were escorted out.

Pike's lawyers filed an emergency motion saying she still had a heartbeat and was audibly snoring after the drugs were given. After 11 p.m., the Tennessee Department of Correction said officials "followed every step" of the protocol, and Pike was taken to an off-site medical facility ([WPLN](https://wpln.org/post/tennessee-set-to-carry-out-rare-execution-of-a-woman/)). Her lawyers' own statement that night cited "difficult vein access, blown veins, degraded pentobarbital," and a lack of emergency care ([Law Dork](https://www.lawdork.com/p/the-big-problems-with-wednesdays)).

Her lawyer Randy Spivey later said that on the Monday night after the attempt ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/news/2026/10/7/christa-pike-was-nearly-taken-off-life-support-before-waking-lawyers-say)), "our understanding was that Christa was brain dead, and life support would be removed the next day." On Tuesday, he said, "Christa woke up and began talking." She asked, "Where am I?" and "Who are you?" He said her arms and hands remain heavily swollen and covered in blisters, and described her as "angry and confused." Experts have speculated that the drug pooled in the tissue of her arm rather than entering a vein, though that is speculation, not a finding (Al Jazeera, above).

Governor Lee, who is leaving office, ordered a third-party review and paused other executions this year ([ABC News/AP](https://abcnews.com/US/wireStory/execute-christa-pike-push-uncharted-legal-territory-experts-137082706)). A state judge ordered the Department of Correction to preserve evidence, including drugs, syringes, IV lines, recordings and communications, and the Corrections Commissioner announced he will resign ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/news/2026/10/7/christa-pike-was-nearly-taken-off-life-support-before-waking-lawyers-say)).

This was not Tennessee's first failure this year. In May, officials called off the execution of Tony Carruthers after more than an hour of failed attempts to place an IV ([WPLN](https://wpln.org/post/tennessee-set-to-carry-out-rare-execution-of-a-woman/)).

## The Question the Court Now Cannot Dodge

Tennessee has not said whether it will try again. If it does, the legal terrain is thin and unsettled, and experts say the litigation could take years ([ABC News/AP](https://abcnews.com/US/wireStory/execute-christa-pike-push-uncharted-legal-territory-experts-137082706)).

The main Supreme Court precedent is *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber* (1947). Willie Francis survived a first attempt in 1946 when an improperly prepared electric chair malfunctioned. A plurality of the Court allowed a second attempt, writing: "Accidents happen for which no man is to blame" ([opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/329/459)). Justice Burton's dissent wrote: "The contrast is that between instantaneous death and death by installments." Francis was electrocuted in 1947.

Experts quoted by the AP say the Court has not revisited that holding under its modern "evolving standards of decency" approach. Jordan Steiker of the University of Texas points out that earlier second-attempt cases mostly involved halts before drugs flowed. Pike bore the full effect of the drugs. In Alabama, Alan Miller and Kenneth Smith both survived failed lethal injections and argued a second attempt would be unconstitutional as applied to them. Early courts found the claims "plausible." The Supreme Court did not take up the question, and both men were later executed with nitrogen gas in 2024 ([ABC News/AP](https://abcnews.com/US/wireStory/execute-christa-pike-push-uncharted-legal-territory-experts-137082706)).

The simplest way out, Steiker says, is commutation, which Governor Lee can grant. If he does not, the question may land at the Court again. And the justices who refused to let one more court look at Pike's case for a few hours will have to decide whether a state may try to kill someone it already failed to kill.

## Why This Is a Court Reform Story

The lesson is not that Christa Pike deserves any particular outcome. It is about process, and process is what the Court has been wearing away.

**Unexplained orders in irreversible cases.** The emergency docket lets the Court decide the most consequential questions in a single unsigned paragraph, with little briefing, no argument and no accountability. When the result is permanent, the absence of reasons is not a technicality. It is a failure of the Court's basic obligation to justify its power.

**Haste as the default.** Executions are scheduled to the hour, and the Court has increasingly treated late appeals as a form of misconduct instead of a symptom of a system that forces litigants to race the clock. The Sixth Circuit's brief pause was not obstruction. It was the ordinary appellate process the Court says it respects.

**No check, no transparency.** The justices who lifted the stay face no ethics enforcement, no required explanation and no appeal. The same Court that has never adopted an enforceable code of conduct did not tell the public why it overrode a lower court in a case about a human life.

Reform would not decide the Pike case for or against her. It would change how decisions like this one are made: requiring written explanations for emergency orders, limiting how the [shadow docket](/posts/shadow-docket-immunity/) can be used, and creating real accountability for the justices who wield it. A Court that must show its work is a Court that is more likely to do it carefully.

On September 30, the Court's majority decided that a short pause for review was too much to give. What the state then did with those hours is on the record.
